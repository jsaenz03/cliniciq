The Clinic Task Run-Sheet: A Daily System That Stops Things Falling Through the Cracks

By John Saenz

Registered Nurse & Founder, ClinicIQ Solutions

The vaccine fridge got checked, but nobody reset the min/max. The recall list exists, but it's on a sticky note in someone's drawer. A run-sheet turns "I thought someone did it" into a checklist with names on it — here's how to build one that survives a busy week.

Ask a practice manager what fell over this month and the answer is almost never a big thing. It's a small recurring thing — a fridge check nobody logged, a patient called about a result but nobody documented the call, a cleaning audit that quietly didn't happen. Small recurring tasks are exactly the ones human memory is worst at, and exactly what a run-sheet is for.

Key takeaways

  • A run-sheet is your recurring daily work written down as a shared checklist — fridge checks, results follow-up, recalls, room resets — with a named owner per task.
  • The five-minute morning huddle is the keystone habit: today's absences, the overdue-task review, and who owns what.
  • Split tasks into daily, weekly and monthly layers so the daily list stays short enough to actually finish.
  • Close the loop on every task — "done" means documented, not just remembered.
  • NursEpod gives the team shared calendars, recurring tasks, team messaging, a policy register and CPD tracking; free for small teams.

Why memory fails at exactly the wrong tasks

Humans are good at one-off, urgent, interesting tasks and reliably poor at frequent, low-stakes, repetitive ones. The fridge check matters more when it's been done 400 days in a row than on any single day — which is precisely why it's the first thing that slips when the day gets busy. Add leave, part-time rosters and locums, and "everyone knows it's their job" quietly becomes nobody's job.

The fix isn't more diligence. It's moving the work out of memory and into a shared system with three properties: every task has one named owner, recurring tasks regenerate on their own schedule, and anyone can see the state of the board at a glance.

The five-minute morning huddle

Run it standing up before the first patient — nurses, reception, and the practice manager if available:

  1. Who's away today? Absences reshuffle ownership; say it out loud.
  2. Overdue tasks. Scan the board. Anything red gets an owner and a deadline before the huddle ends — not a shrug.
  3. Today's specifics. Dressing changes booked, Drs running corporate health assessments, the vaccine delivery arriving, the air-conditioner tech coming.
  4. One safety beat. Yesterday's results that need chasing, a recall that bounced, a cold-chain reading that was borderline.

Five minutes, same time, every day. Practices that huddle reliably catch things while they're still cheap to fix.

The daily run-sheet

Keep the daily layer short — ten to fifteen items across the whole team:

  • Opening: vaccine fridge min/max check and reset (twice daily — this is the morning one), treatment rooms stocked and reset, computers and phones on, emergency exits clear.
  • Through the day: results reviewed and actioned as they arrive; incoming pathology tracked; recalls and reminders for the day sent; messages relayed to GPs between patients.
  • Closing: second fridge check, consulting rooms restocked, sharps below the fill line, tomorrow's chasing list written, locked up.

Each item gets a name, not a department. "Reception does the fridge" fails; "Priya does the fridge, Marcus covers Wednesdays" works.

The weekly and monthly layers

Everything else lives on its own schedule so it never clutters the daily list: equipment calibration and service dates, the cleaning audit, the emergency drug box expiry check, policy reviews, staff CPD records, and the monthly stock take (we've written a full checklist for that separately). This layer is also where a practice's policy register earns its keep — when the policy lives next to the task it governs, reviews actually happen instead of being promised.

Close the loop, in writing

A task isn't done when it's done — it's done when it's documented. The fridge check isn't the reading; it's the reading written in the log with initials. The patient call isn't the call; it's the note in the record. Run-sheets fail when ticking the box and doing the work drift apart, so keep the tick and the evidence in the same place.

Frequently asked questions

What is a clinic task run-sheet?

A run-sheet is the set of recurring tasks a practice must complete every day — fridge checks, results follow-up, recalls, room resets, equipment checks — written down as a shared checklist with a named owner for each item, rather than living in individual people's memories.

Who can assign tasks in NursEpod?

Any member of the practice team can create and assign tasks in NursEpod — practice managers and senior nurses typically own the recurring run-sheet, while GPs and nurses can raise tasks for each other without needing a meeting. Every task has one named owner so accountability is clear.

Does NursEpod work on the clinic iPad?

Yes. NursEpod runs in the browser, so it works on the clinic iPad, the reception PC or a phone — wherever the team already works. Tasks ticked off in the treatment room show up instantly for the practice manager.

How do we stop tasks being forgotten when someone is on leave?

Two habits: every recurring task needs a named owner plus a delegate, and an open-task review at the morning huddle surfaces anything overdue before it becomes urgent. A shared task board makes leave cover a handover, not an amnesia event.

Vaccine Fridge Management in GP Clinics: A Practical System for Australian Practices

By John Saenz

Registered Nurse & Founder, ClinicIQ Solutions

Vaccine fridge management comes down to five jobs: one named owner, a written policy, live stock records, temperature monitoring and a scheduled review. What the standards actually require, what poor fridge management costs, and where software earns its keep.

Vaccine fridge management in a GP clinic comes down to five jobs: one named person who owns the fridge, a written cold-chain policy, live stock records with expiry tracking, continuous temperature monitoring, and a scheduled review of all of it. Do those five consistently and the fridge stops being a source of quiet loss and audit anxiety.

Most practices already have pieces of this — a min/max diary, a data logger, someone who "does the fridge". The gap between pieces and a system is where money leaks: expired doses discovered at the worst moment, breach stock-takes that swallow an afternoon, and reordering from memory before flu season. This article sets out what Australian practices are actually required to do, the five jobs in order, and where software earns its keep.

Key takeaways

  • Cold chain is an explicit accreditation criterion (GP6.1, RACGP Standards 5th edition): a named owner, compliance with 'Strive for 5', reviewed processes and a written, practice-specific policy.
  • Australian vaccines worth $25.9 million were lost to cold chain breaches between 2014 and 2019 — and in 2025 alone, WA breach wastage exceeded $600,000 in government-funded vaccines.
  • Temperature monitoring and stock management are separate jobs: a logger knows nothing about batches and expiries; a stock system knows nothing about degrees.
  • Accreditation under the NGPA Scheme currently uses the 5th edition; practices are encouraged to begin implementing the 6th (ACSQHC, verified 17 November 2026) — the substance of GP6.1 will survive any edition change.

What the standards actually require

Cold chain is an explicit accreditation criterion. Under the RACGP Standards for general practices (5th edition), criterion GP6.1 — Maintaining vaccine potency — requires practices to (AGPAL):

  • GP6.1 A — have at least one team member with primary responsibility for cold chain management;
  • GP6.1 B — ensure the process used complies with the current edition of the National Vaccine Storage Guidelines ('Strive for 5');
  • GP6.1 C — review the processes that keep stock potent: ordering and stock rotation protocols, equipment maintenance, an annual audit of vaccine storage procedures, continuity of the cold chain including handover between designated team members, and the accuracy of the fridge thermometer; and
  • GP6.1 D — hold a written, practice-specific policy outlining the cold chain processes.

On editions, the current position (verified 17 November 2026): the RACGP published the 6th edition in 2026 and the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care encourages practices to begin implementing it, but accreditation under the National General Practice Accreditation Scheme currently uses the 5th edition (ACSQHC). Check the ACSQHC page for the transition date before you plan your assessment cycle — and note that GP6.1's substance is unlikely to go away: a named owner, a current policy, and reviewed stock processes will survive any edition change.

'Strive for 5' itself is the national standard behind the criterion: vaccines stored at +2°C to +8°C, aiming for 5°C. Our vaccine cold-chain checklist covers the daily temperature routine; this article covers the stock and system side around it.

What poor fridge management actually costs

The losses are measured, not hypothetical:

  • $25.9 million in Australian vaccines lost to cold chain breaches between 2014 and 2019, across more than 12,000 recorded breaches and at least 747,000 destroyed doses — about 1.65% of all vaccines distributed (RACGP newsGP, 2019). The reporting itself flagged that incomplete jurisdictional statistics mean the true figure is likely higher.
  • In 2025 alone, WA Public Health Units advised on 933 cold chain breaches, and 409 of them resulted in wastage of government-funded vaccines worth more than $600,000 (WA Health, Vaccine Update 471, 2025).

Cold chain breaches are only half the loss, though. The other half is quieter: short-dated stock that expires on the shelf because nobody saw it coming, duplicate orders placed because the fridge contents were unknown, and staff hours burned on manual counts. One number shows how much process matters: in NSW, after a structured breach-assessment procedure was introduced in 2019, the average value of vaccines lost per breach fell from about $4,500 in 2018 to about $2,300 in 2019 (newsGP, 2019). Better process halved the average loss. That is the argument for a system rather than good intentions.

The five jobs of vaccine fridge management

  1. Name the owner. GP6.1 A is not bureaucratic box-ticking — fridges fail when everyone assumes someone else is watching. The owner is usually a practice nurse; they hold the policy, run the reviews, and are the escalation point for breaches. Name a deputy for leave coverage; 'Strive for 5' expects continuity of the cold chain, including handover between designated team members.
  2. Keep the policy written and current. GP6.1 D asks for a written, practice-specific policy — specific to your fridges, your staff, your after-hours arrangements. If your policy still describes a fridge you replaced two years ago, it is an audit liability, not an asset.
  3. Run live stock records. This is the job most practices do worst, because a spreadsheet or a diary note is always out of date. You need to know, at any moment: what is in the fridge, which batches, which expire first, and what is at or below reorder level. This is also what makes the GP6.1 C reviews real — ordering and stock rotation protocols are evidence when they are practised, not when they are laminated.
  4. Monitor temperature continuously — and separately. Twice-daily min/max logging plus a data logger reviewed at least weekly is the 'Strive for 5' baseline. Note what this job is not: it is not stock management. A temperature logger knows nothing about batches and expiries; a stock system knows nothing about degrees. Practices get into trouble when they assume one covers the other.
  5. Review on a schedule. The annual audit GP6.1 C requires is the floor, not the goal. A short quarterly review — expiries, reorder levels, policy currency, logger data — takes fifteen minutes and means the annual audit is a formality instead of an archaeology project.

What a vaccine fridge management system looks like in practice

Pull the five jobs onto one page and the daily reality looks like this:

  • The fridge opens, stock comes out for a patient, someone scans it against the stock record — ten seconds, and the count stays true.
  • The expiring-soon list is checked on Monday mornings. Anything inside 90 days goes on a use-first plan or the reorder decision is made consciously.
  • The low-stock list drives ordering. Flu-season orders are built from reorder levels set months earlier, not from someone recalling what the fridge looked like last winter.
  • Temperature logs and the logger printout live in their own routine, run by the same named owner, per the cold-chain checklist.
  • When the accreditation surveyor asks how the practice maintains vaccine potency, the named owner demonstrates the policy, the records, and the review cycle — the evidence exists because the system runs, not because someone assembled it the week before.

Getting started: a realistic first month

Week 1 — name the owner and deputy; print the current policy and date it; run one full stock-take (a printable template exists if you are starting on paper).

Week 2 — set up live stock records and QR labels; set expiry alert thresholds (90 days works for most practices) and reorder levels per product.

Week 3 — establish the Monday expiring-soon check and the low-stock ordering routine; make sure the deputy knows the breach procedure cold.

Week 4 — hold the first quarterly review: expiries seen, orders placed, policy current, logger data checked. Fifteen minutes. Book the next one.

From there, the annual audit GP6.1 asks for is a read-out of a system that has been running all year — not a scramble.

Frequently asked questions

Who is responsible for vaccine cold chain in a GP practice?

The practice must have at least one team member with primary responsibility for cold chain management (criterion GP6.1 A of the RACGP Standards for general practices, 5th edition). In most practices this is a practice nurse, with a named deputy for leave coverage. Responsibility for the system sits with that person; everyone who opens the fridge shares the daily duty of care.

How often should a GP clinic do a vaccine fridge stock-take?

A full stock-take at least annually satisfies the audit expectation in GP6.1 C, but practices running live stock records do better with a full count quarterly plus the weekly expiring-soon check. The goal is that no expired or short-dated dose is ever discovered by using it.

What temperature should a vaccine fridge be set at?

Vaccines are stored at +2°C to +8°C, aiming for 5°C — the midpoint that gives the biggest buffer on both sides of the range, per the National Vaccine Storage Guidelines ('Strive for 5'). Many vaccines are damaged or destroyed at temperatures outside the range.

Does software replace our temperature logger?

No — and be wary of anything that blurs this. Temperature monitoring (logger, twice-daily min/max checks, weekly data review) and stock management (batches, expiries, ordering) are separate jobs under 'Strive for 5' and GP6.1. cIQventory handles the stock side; the logger remains your temperature system of record.

Is cold chain management part of GP accreditation?

Yes. Vaccine potency is an explicit criterion (GP6.1) in the RACGP Standards for general practices, and accreditation under the National General Practice Accreditation Scheme currently runs against the 5th edition (ACSQHC, verified November 2026). A documented cold chain system with named ownership is standard evidence — though no tool or template can guarantee an accreditation outcome; surveyors assess the practice's own demonstrated processes.

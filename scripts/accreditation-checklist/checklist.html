Section H: Inventory your AI tools

Most practices use more AI than they can name from memory. A scribe that drafts the consult note. Inbox triage. Phone-message and letter summaries. A search box that answers like a person. None of it arrives labelled “AI governance required” — it arrives because it saves time, and it spreads one desk at a time.

AGPAL’s published webinar takeaways on the updated Standards (8 September 2026) put a practical first step on the table: identify the AI-enabled tools already in use across the practice, and ask of each one — what information goes in, when patient consent is required, who is responsible for reviewing what comes out, and what safeguards, training, risk assessment and monitoring sit around it.

To be straight with you, as the rest of this checklist is: today’s survey does not assess this section. The value is threefold anyway. The questions it forces you to answer — what data goes where, who checks the output, who told the patient — are the same privacy and safety questions your current obligations already ask. And an inventory maintained on real dates is exactly the kind of evidence this checklist keeps coming back to: proof of use, not proof of intention.

Same rules as everywhere else in this checklist. A table nobody maintains is not evidence. An inventory updated on real dates, with names in it, is.

Checking your inventory is real

Tick an item only when you can show the evidence today.

The practice keeps a written inventory of every AI-enabled tool in use, including AI features inside ordinary software (inbox triage, phone and message summarisation, letter drafts, search assistants)
Each row says what data goes in, in plain words: the consultation conversation? pasted clinical details? nothing identifiable?
For each tool, the consent position is decided and recorded — yes, no, or unsure with a note on what would settle it
Each tool names the human who reviews its outputs before anything reaches the record or the patient — a role and a name, not “the team”
Safeguards are written down for each tool, and the staff who use the tool know them: vendor and privacy assessment before adoption, access limits, what to do when an output is wrong
Where consent is required, patients are told before the tool is used, the consent is recorded, and a tool-free alternative is genuinely offered
New tools get a row before their first patient-facing use, not after
The inventory is re-checked on the dates in the last column, and any AI-related incident or near miss lands in the incident register and triggers a row update
The privacy policy matches what the inventory says the practice actually does

Reading your results in this section

Blank rows, or a tool with no named reviewer? A finding, and usually fixable in an afternoon. Fill the row before the tool is used again.

“Unsure” in the consent column? Not a failure — an honest row beats a confident guess. Settle it against the privacy policy and the vendor’s data-flow information, or bring the row to a readiness assessment and work it through there.

A tool that touches results, recalls or high-risk pathways? The failsafe six apply to those pathways no matter what the row says. Fix the failsafe first.

Bringing this inventory to a readiness assessment is the fastest way to turn it from a table into governance: every row gets checked for the questions a surveyor would ask next. Book at cliniciq.com.au/booking.html.
